Learn Solopreneur with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Your hours are the ceiling

A business of one starts by selling the owner's time, and time has a hard top: there are only so many hours in a week, and not all of them can be sold. Tonight we count how many businesses like this exist and why the surveys of what they earn disagree so badly, then open one constructed consultant's year and find the hours that never reach an invoice. We finish with the arithmetic every solo business lives under, price times billable hours, and the one number that tells you what an hour of your week is actually worth.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Marlow is an invented solo data consultant who left an analytics team two years ago and now works alone: dashboards, data clean-ups and the occasional forecast for small companies. Marlow sells by the hour at $110. One anchor client sends 14 to 22 hours a week, and a handful of small clients send requests of four to eight hours. Marlow can work about 45 hours a week before the work, or Marlow, starts to break. Every number about Marlow comes from a seeded model that session 4 lets you run, and every page says so. Illustrative, not financial or tax advice.

Part 1 · covers how many solo businesses exist, and what they earn

Three counts, three different nets

5 min live

Before Marlow's year, the population Marlow belongs to. Official statistics count businesses with nobody on the payroll; a survey company counts people who earn "independently". The numbers look like they answer one question. They answer three, because each source drops a different net into a different sea.

LiveWhat the official counts say, and what they do not3 min▶

The US Census Bureau counts nonemployer businesses: businesses with no paid employees, subject to federal income tax, with receipts of $1,000 or more ($1 or more in construction). The data come from tax records. In 2022 there were 29.8 million of them with $1.7 trillion in receipts. Divide one by the other and you get about $57,000 of receipts per business. That is our arithmetic, not a Census figure, and it is a mean of receipts: money in before any costs, pulled up by a minority of large ones, and including many people for whom the business is a side income.

In the UK, the Department for Business and Trade's business population estimates put 5.5 million private sector businesses at the start of 2024, of which 4.1 million, 74 percent, employed nobody aside from the owners. From the release's own table, those businesses had about 6.9 percent of all private sector turnover (our arithmetic; the table leaves out finance and insurance). Most businesses are businesses of one; most turnover is not.

Receipts and turnover are not profit, and profit is not cash. That distinction belongs to Finance ↗; here the point is only that none of these numbers is anyone's take-home pay.

Self-studyWhy "solopreneur income" surveys disagree3 min▶

MBO Partners, a company that sells services to independent workers, publishes an annual State of Independence report. Its 2026 page headlines "a record 74.9M Americans now earn income independently" and says "almost 6M of them now earn more than $100K per year". The page does not describe how the independent-workforce survey was run. Those figures count people, not businesses, by the survey's own definition of independence, and the headline picks the high earners.

None of this makes the vendor wrong, and none of it makes the Census mean right about you. The honest position is the disagreement itself: a tax-record mean of about $57,000 of receipts, a UK register where three in four businesses have no staff and share a small slice of turnover, and a vendor survey that celebrates the top. When someone quotes "solopreneurs earn X", ask three questions: who was counted, is it receipts or profit, and does the publisher sell to the people it counted.

Real world

The side business inside the statistic. A nonemployer count includes the teacher who tutors at weekends, the engineer who sells a few templates and the full-time consultant alike, because all of them file business income with nobody on the payroll. Read any average across that population as a description of a mixture, not of a career. The rest of the course is about the full-time end: one person whose hours are the business.

Part 2 · covers the hours that are not billable

The hours that never reach an invoice

5 min live

Marlow's capacity is 45 hours a week, 2,340 hours across 52 weeks. In the modelled year with only hourly work, Marlow works 1,717 of them and bills 1,313. The gap between available, worked and billed is the first thing a business of one has to see clearly, because it is invisible on any invoice.

LiveFour kinds of hours, only one of them paid3 min▶

Every hour of a solo week falls into one of four kinds. Billable hours are delivered to a client and invoiced. Sales hours win the work: scoping calls, proposals, follow-ups. Admin hours keep the business running: invoices, bookkeeping, email, tools, tax paperwork. Building hours make something you will sell later, a template, a course, a packaged offer. Only the first kind is paid this week.

In Marlow's modelled all-hourly year, admin is 370 hours, about seven a week, made of a fixed six hours plus half an hour for every client billed that week. More small clients means more admin, which is why the small requests cost more than their invoices show. Sales is only 34 hours because the anchor client needs no selling. Billable hours are 76.5 percent of the hours worked.

Self-studyWhy the top bar is not full2 min▶

Marlow worked 1,717 of 2,340 available hours, about 33 hours a week. The model never made Marlow refuse to work; there simply was not enough hourly demand most weeks. That is a common shape for a business that sells only time: capacity sits idle in quiet weeks and cannot be stored for busy ones. An hour not sold on Tuesday is gone. Session 2's offer ladder is largely a way of selling things that do not expire on Tuesday.

Where your own week goes, and how to triage it, is taught in AI Productivity ↗. Here the hours matter only as entries on a business ledger.

Count admin per client, not per week. If each new client adds a fixed slice of invoicing, email and onboarding, a $400 request can cost an hour you never see on the request. Price small work with that slice in it, or batch it.

Part 3 · covers the ceiling and the effective rate

Price times billable hours, and nothing else

5 min live

A business that sells hours has exactly two levers on revenue: the price of an hour and the number of hours billed. Both have a top. Multiply the two tops together and you have the ceiling, the most this way of selling can ever earn.

LiveThe effective rate is the honest number3 min▶

Marlow's invoices say $110 an hour. Divide the year's revenue, $144,430, by every hour Marlow worked, 1,717, and each hour of the week earned $84.12. The difference is the admin and sales nobody paid for. The effective rate, revenue divided by all hours, is the number to compare across offers, across years and against a salary, because it charges every offer for the work it drags in with it.

The ceiling is simpler and harsher. At 45 hours a week for 52 weeks and $110 an hour, the most hourly work can bring in is $257,400, and only if every available hour were billed, which no week ever is. The modelled year reaches 56 percent of it.

Self-studyWhy working more barely moves it3 min▶

On the session 4 bench, raise Marlow's weekly capacity from 45 to 55 hours on the all-hourly rung and revenue moves from $144,430 to $147,620. Ten more hours a week of willingness buy almost nothing, because the limit in most weeks was demand, not hours. Raise the hourly price from $110 to $150 instead and revenue moves to $196,950 in the model, which assumes the same clients keep the same hours at the higher price. Whether they would is a question about demand and price response that Economics ↗ owns; the model does not know.

Either way, both levers stay inside the same ceiling. A higher price lifts the line; it does not remove it. Removing it takes a different thing to sell.

Real world

The freelancer who doubled their hours. A familiar pattern: a solo designer, busy and short of money, starts working evenings and weekends. Revenue rises for a quarter, then falls, because the extra hours went to the lowest-paying work that was available at short notice, and the admin of the extra clients ate the rest. Session 4's anti-lever runs that exact move on Marlow and prints what happens.

Build-along

Your hour ledger, one real week

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take last week from your calendar, or the week you remember best. You will sort every working hour into four kinds, compute your effective rate and your ceiling, and name the largest block of unpaid work. If you are not yet selling anything, use Marlow's numbers from the prompts and do the same arithmetic.

List every working block from the week. From the calendar, sent mail and invoices. Round to the half hour. Do not sort yet: the first job is to get the hours that were not in the calendar, such as the evening spent on an invoice dispute.

★ Step 1 · raw blocksMon 09:00-12:30 anchor client dashboard 3.5 Mon 13:30-14:00 invoices + chasing payment 0.5 Mon 14:00-15:00 scoping call, new small client 1.0 ... Fri 16:00-17:30 tax receipts folder 1.5

Sort each block into one of four kinds. If a block was two kinds, split it. Travel to a client counts with the work it served.

★ Step 2 · four rowsbillable 23.0 sales 2.5 admin 7.5 building 0.0 total 33.0

Compute the effective rate. The week's revenue, invoiced or earned, divided by the total row. Write the list rate next to it.

★ Step 3 · effective raterevenue this week 23.0 h x $110 = $2,530 effective rate $2,530 / 33.0 h = $76.67 per hour worked list rate $110 per hour billed (your numbers will differ)

Compute your ceiling. Your realistic weekly capacity, times the weeks you will actually work in a year, times your price. Say out loud what fraction of it last week reached.

★ Step 4 · the ceilingcapacity 45 h a week weeks 52 (Marlow); most people work fewer, use yours price $110 ceiling 45 x 52 x $110 = $257,400 last week $2,530 x 52 = $131,560 a year, 51% of the ceiling

Name the biggest unpaid block. Which non-billable row is largest, and is it fixed per week or does it grow with each client? That one sentence is the input to session 2.

★ Step 5 · the findingBiggest unpaid row: admin, 7.5 h. About 4 h of it is per client (onboarding, invoices, email for three small clients). Small requests cost more than their invoices show.

Write the one-line diagnosis. Price-limited, demand-limited or hours-limited? Price-limited means you are full and cheap; demand-limited means you have idle hours; hours-limited means you are full and fairly priced, and only a different offer will help.

★ Step 6 · diagnosisMarlow, all hourly: demand-limited. 33 of 45 hours worked, idle capacity in most weeks, and the anchor client is most of the revenue (session 5 comes back to that).

Keep the ledger for four weeks. One week can be an outlier. Four weeks show whether admin is a fixed tax or a per-client one, and session 6 turns the ledger into a weekly fifteen-minute review.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A headline says "the average solo business earns $57,000". Using the Census figures, what is the most accurate reading?

The Census reports the count and the receipts; the division is arithmetic, and the result is a mean of money in, not profit and not take-home pay. A and B misread it. D overreaches: different sources count different populations, which is the disagreement to keep, not resolve.

2 · Marlow invoices $110 an hour, earned $144,430 in the modelled year and worked 1,717 hours. What does the effective rate of $84.12 tell you?

Revenue divided by every hour worked, not just billed ones. It is not profit (no costs are taken off) and it does not suggest a price. It is the number to compare offers by, because every offer drags in its own unpaid work.

3 · In the model, raising Marlow's capacity from 45 to 55 hours a week on the all-hourly rung lifts revenue from $144,430 to only $147,620. Why?

The all-hourly year used about 33 of 45 hours a week. A demand-limited business does not grow by offering more hours; it needs more demand, a higher price, or something else to sell. The ceiling is never reached, so it is not what binds here.
