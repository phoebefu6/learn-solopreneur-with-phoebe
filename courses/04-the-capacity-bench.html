The capacity bench

Sessions 1 to 3 argued that a business of one hits a ceiling made of hours, that some offers sell something other than hours, and that leverage moves work off the clock. Tonight the argument runs. Marlow's year is a seeded 52-week model in this page: demand arrives every week, a stated booking rule decides what Marlow takes on inside 45 hours, and the page prints revenue, every hour by kind, the effective rate, the weeks over capacity and how much of the year one client paid for. You climb the ladder rung by rung, then try the move that feels like growth, then take away the biggest client in week 27 and see what is left.

Part 0

Where we start

Nothing on this bench happened. Marlow is constructed, and the year is a model: the same seeded draw of demand every time, so the only thing that changes between rungs is what Marlow sells. That makes the bench good for comparing ways of running a business of one, and useless for predicting yours. Every number carries a "modelled" tag, and an independent Python run reproduces every default number on this page to the cent. Illustrative, not financial or tax advice.

Part 1 · covers how the model books a week

A 45-hour bucket, filled in a fixed order

6 min live

Each modelled week starts empty. Fixed commitments go in first, then the work with the most value per hour, and hourly requests last, each only if what it needs fits in what is left. Admin is charged as a base plus a slice for every client billed that week, so taking on a new client costs hours before any work is done.

LiveFive outputs, and why the third is the one to watch3 min▶

Annual revenue is every invoice in the year. Total hours counts all four kinds from session 1: billable, sales, admin and building. Effective hourly rate divides the first by the second, so it charges each offer for the selling, admin and building it drags in. Weeks over capacity counts weeks above 45 hours, which the booking rule avoids unless you switch it off. Top client's share is the fraction of the year's revenue that one client paid; session 5 is about that number.

Revenue is the number a business of one is tempted to optimise. The effective rate is the one that tells you whether a new offer is better or just bigger, and the top client's share tells you how fragile the year is. The bench prints all three side by side so no rung can win on one while hiding the others.

Self-studyThe defaults, and what they assume3 min▶

Hourly work is $110 an hour. The anchor client asks for 14 to 22 hours a week; zero to three small requests of four to eight hours arrive each week from six small clients. A fixed-scope project is $5,400 for four weeks of nine hours, takes five hours to win, and Marlow runs at most two at once. The productized offer is $1,800 for seven hours of delivery and one of sales, after eight weeks of building it at eight hours a week; it sells up to two a week from week 9. Retainers are $700 a week for five hours, take eight hours to win, and are won in weeks 5, 14 and 30. Admin is six hours a week plus half an hour per client billed. Automation cuts admin by 40 percent for a 20-hour set-up in week 1. Weekly costs are $2,000 and Marlow starts with $8,000 of cash.

The biggest assumption is that demand does not respond to anything Marlow does: the same requests arrive whatever the price and whatever else Marlow sells. That makes rungs comparable and is plainly false about any real market. Price response belongs to Economics ↗.

Build-along · the bench

Marlow's year, rung by rung

★ 26 min · the main event

Start on rung 1 and read the verdict line, then the six numbers, then the 52 bars against the dashed capacity line. Move one rung at a time and say out loud which of the three headline numbers moved and which way. Only then try the two red buttons. The sliders change capacity and the hourly price; the numbers quoted on this page are all at the defaults, 45 hours and $110.

Read it in this order. Verdict, effective rate, top client's share, then revenue. On rung 1 Marlow earns $144,430 for 1,717 hours, an effective $84.12 an hour, and the anchor client pays 69.5 percent of the year. Keep those three numbers in your head as you climb.

Part 2 · covers what the ladder does

Two numbers up the ladder

6 min live

These are the numbers the widget prints at the defaults, written down so they can be argued with after class. The Python reference reproduces every one of them.

LiveRungs 2 and 3: selling outcomes, then packages2 min▶

Adding fixed-scope projects takes revenue from $144,430 to $206,940 and the effective rate from $84.12 to $96.32. Projects fill the idle hours that hourly demand left empty, at $150 for each delivery hour, and they spread revenue across more clients: the anchor client's share falls from 69.5 to 46.9 percent. Adding the productized offer costs 64 hours of building in weeks 1 to 8 and earns nothing in those weeks, yet by year end revenue is $250,530 and the rate $110.93, the first rung above the $110 list price. A package sold off the shelf earns more per hour than the hour it replaces because its sales and scoping were done once, not per client.

LiveRung 4: the rung that earns less per hour2 min▶

Retainers add $1,390 of revenue over rung 3 and lower the effective rate from $110.93 to $108.80, because they book before projects and productized units and displace some of that higher-value work. On rate alone, rung 4 is a step back. On concentration it is the biggest step forward: the top client's share falls from 30.8 to 18.2 percent, and the revenue lost when that client walks halves. A retainer sells access, and access is predictable. Whether that is worth $2.13 an hour depends on how much you value a calmer year, which no model can say for you.

Self-studyRung 5: what automation buys on this ledger2 min▶

Cutting admin by 40 percent for 20 hours of set-up takes admin from 434.5 to 263.7 hours and the rate to $116.43. Notice where the freed hours went: billable hours rose from 1,689 to 1,794, because the booking rule refilled them with work that had been turned away. Automation did not shorten Marlow's year much; it converted unpaid hours into paid ones. How to build the automations is taught in Automation ↗; here they are only hours moved on the ledger.

Real world

The consultant who would not take a retainer. A common objection: "a retainer pays me less per hour than my projects do." On the bench that is exactly right, and it is also why the objection misses the point. The retainer is not competing with the project on rate. It is competing with the empty week after the project ends, and with the week a big client leaves. Switch on the break button at rung 3, then at rung 4, and compare the holes.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

The move that feels like growth, and the week it all goes

6 min live

Two red buttons. The first is the most natural reaction to a quiet month: take everything. The second is the event every business of one should rehearse: the biggest client stops. Both are modelled by a stated rule, and the widget prints the rule next to the button.

LiveThe anti-lever: say yes to every hourly client2 min▶

The rule: every hourly request is taken whatever the week already holds, and so are discount requests at $85 an hour that the normal rule turns away. On rung 1, revenue rises from $144,430 to $171,420, but hours rise faster, from 1,717 to 2,089.5, so the effective rate falls from $84.12 to $82.04. Fifteen weeks go over 45 hours and the busiest reaches 64.5. Every new small client brings its own sales and admin slice, which is why the extra revenue earns less than the existing work.

The honest nuance: on rung 1 the revenue gain is real, about $27,000, and in a cash emergency it can be the right call for a quarter. On rung 5 the same button lifts revenue from $265,320 to $385,370, which looks spectacular until you read the hours: 3,536.4 in the year, 68 a week on average, 50 weeks over capacity, and a rate down from $116.43 to $108.97. The model has no burnout, no quality loss and no clients who notice. Real ones do.

LiveThe break button: lose the biggest client2 min▶

A bench that can only produce good news is measuring nothing. The break button finds the client with the most revenue in the same run, which is the anchor client on every rung, and stops all its work from week 27. On rung 1 the year loses $48,290, revenue after the loss falls to $935 a week against $2,000 of weekly costs, and the cash built up by week 26 lasts about 26 weeks. From rung 2 up, what is left still covers costs, and the hole shrinks every rung: $36,850, $24,640, $11,550, $9,130.

One detail is worth seeing: in the model, part of the hole refills on its own, because the hours the anchor client freed are taken by small requests that had been turned away. That is realistic, and it is also why runway, not revenue, is the number to rehearse. Session 5 turns this into a monthly check.

Self-studyWhat the model cannot tell you2 min▶

Demand never responds to price or reputation. Clients never leave unless you press the button. There are no taxes, no late payers, no holidays and no illness, and Marlow works the same 52 weeks every year. Each of those would change the numbers, and some would change the order of the rungs. What survives them is the direction: selling something other than hours lifts the effective rate and spreads the risk, and taking every hour on offer does not. Treat any single number here as a picture of a mechanism, never as a forecast.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Adding retainers (rung 4) lifts revenue by $1,390 but lowers the effective rate from $110.93 to $108.80. What is the best reading?

The bench prints two numbers so a rung cannot win on one and hide the other. Rung 4 is the rung where they disagree, and the honest answer names the trade. A and D each read one number; C assumes a ladder must be monotone, which real choices are not.

2 · On rung 1, saying yes to every hourly client raises revenue from $144,430 to $171,420. Why is it the anti-lever?

Revenue rises; that is what makes the move tempting. The rate and the week both get worse, which the bench prints. Nothing here is measured on real people (B), and late payment is not in the model (C).

3 · The break button on rung 1 prints a revenue hole of $48,290 and about 26 weeks of runway. What does "runway" mean here?

Cash at week 26 is $27,830; revenue after the loss is $935 a week against $2,000 of costs. The cash covers the shortfall for about 26 weeks. The costs and the cash are constructed, so the number illustrates a mechanism, not a benchmark.
