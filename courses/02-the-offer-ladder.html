Part 1 · covers the five offer types

Five things you can sell

5 min live

Marlow's homework list from session 1 was short: everything was "by the hour". That is one rung of five. Each rung sells something different, and each is capped by something different. Climb, and the tie between what you earn and the hours you spend gets looser.

LiveWhat each rung really sells3 min▶

Hourly sells time. The client buys your hours and carries every risk of how many are needed. It is capped by the hours you have.

Fixed-scope sells an outcome for a set fee: "a cleaned customer table and a churn dashboard, $5,400". The client buys a result. It is capped by the scope you agreed, and if the scope leaks, the cap is your own rate (Part 2).

Productized sells the same package every time, at a published price. The first one costs you design hours; the tenth one costs you almost only delivery. It is capped by the package: you can only sell what the package does.

Retainer sells access over a period: "five hours a week of data support, $700 a week". The client buys availability and continuity. It is capped by your calendar, because every retainer reserves part of every week.

Asset sells a thing many times: a template, a course, a tool. Once made, a copy costs you nothing in hours. It is capped by nothing per copy, and by everything about demand.

Most solo businesses do not pick one rung. They stand on two or three, and the useful question is which rung each piece of work belongs on.

Self-studyThe asset rung, and why the bench leaves it out3 min▶

The asset rung is the one people dream about and the one that is hardest to plan. Its income is uncertain, because nobody knows how many copies will sell, and slow, because an audience has to find it first. The building hours are paid up front and the return, if it comes, arrives over months or years.

For that reason the session 4 bench does not model an asset at all. Any number it printed for template or course sales would be invented, and an invented number that looks like a forecast is worse than none. The bench stops at retainers; the asset rung stays in the picture as a direction, not a line on the ledger. Session 3 comes back to it as leverage: things that can be copied without asking anyone's permission.

Real world

Three rungs in one inbox. A familiar pattern: an independent bookkeeper bills one client by the hour for messy catch-up work, does year-end packs for a fixed fee, and keeps two small firms on a monthly arrangement. Nobody planned it as a ladder. Naming the rungs is what lets them see which of the three earns the most per hour of their week, and which one quietly costs them evenings.

Part 2 · covers fixed-scope risk

The scope line

5 min live

The first step up the ladder moves a risk from the client to you. On hourly work, if a job takes longer, the client pays for the extra hours. On fixed-scope work, the fee is the fee: the extra hours are yours, and they come straight out of your rate.

LiveWho absorbs the overrun3 min▶

Marlow's modelled fixed-scope project is $5,400 for 4 weeks of 9 hours: 36 delivery hours, or $150 per delivery hour, well above the $110 hourly price. That premium is what the client pays for certainty. The catch is the denominator. If the same project runs to 45 hours, the fee does not move and the delivery rate falls to $120. That is arithmetic on the model's defaults, not something the bench measured, but it is the same arithmetic every fixed fee lives under.

So a fixed-scope offer transfers one specific thing to you: the risk that the work takes longer than planned. You are paid more per planned hour because you agreed to carry that risk. Whether it was a good trade is decided by how well you drew the edge of the job.

Self-studyFour parts of a scope line4 min▶

A scope line is a short written edge around the job. It has four parts, and each one closes a different leak.

  • Inclusions. What you will deliver, in nouns the client can count: "one churn dashboard with four views, one cleaned customer table".
  • Exclusions. What a reasonable client might assume is in, and is not: "new data sources, user training, changes after sign-off".
  • A change rule. What happens when something new is asked for: "new requests are quoted separately, or swapped for an item of equal size".
  • A done test. The moment the job is finished, written so both sides can see it: "dashboard live, walked through on one call, client confirms in writing".

A scope line is a working agreement, not a contract. For contract terms, liability or payment law, take local professional advice.

Real world

The quick extra chart. A familiar pattern: halfway through a fixed-fee dashboard, the client asks for "one quick extra chart". It is quick. So is the next one, and the filter after it, and the export to slides. None of them is worth an argument on its own, and together they are the nine hours that took the project from $150 to $120 a delivery hour. The change rule exists so that the first quick chart gets a polite answer: happy to, here is what it swaps out or what it costs.

Part 3 · covers productized and retainer trade-offs, and Maister (reported)

Productized and retainer: what each one costs you

5 min live

The two middle rungs both look like pure upside on a slide. Each has a price that is paid before the upside arrives: a packaged offer costs building hours that earn nothing, and a retainer costs some of your rate in exchange for something that is not money.

LiveProductized: hours up front, then the same thing every time3 min▶

In the model, Marlow's productized offer takes 64 building hours, 8 hours a week across weeks 1 to 8, and those hours earn nothing. From week 9 it sells at $1,800 for 7 delivery hours plus 1 hour of sales, at most two a week. The building hours are the price of entry; every later sale is cheaper to deliver because the design work is already done.

The condition is strict: a package only works if it is the same every time. If each buyer needs it bent a little, you are back to fixed-scope with a fixed price and no scope line. The test is your inbox. If clients keep asking for the same audit, the same clean-up, the same review, that request is already a product; you just have not written it down yet.

LiveRetainer: the two-number rung3 min▶

Marlow's modelled retainers are $700 a week for 5 hours of access, and each one takes 8 hours to win. On the session 4 bench, adding retainers on top of the productized rung lifts the year's revenue by only $1,390, and the effective rate actually falls, from $110.93 to $108.80. Judged on one number, it is a step backwards.

Judged on two, it is not. The same step cuts the top client's share of revenue from 30.8 percent to 18.2 percent. A retainer buys spread: more of the year rests on more than one client, so losing the biggest one hurts less. Whether that is worth $2.13 an hour is your decision, and it depends on how exposed you are, which session 5 measures. Those numbers are all modelled. The honest lesson is the trade, not "retainers always win".

Self-studyThe leverage a business of one does not have3 min▶

David Maister's Managing the Professional Service Firm (1993) is reported, in summaries we read rather than the book itself, to describe leverage in professional firms as the ratio of junior to senior professionals: seniors win and shape the work, juniors do much of it, and the firm earns on the hours of people other than the partner. The finders, minders and grinders labels come from the same reported account.

A business of one has no juniors. Every hour delivered is the owner's hour, so the people version of leverage is simply not available. The offer ladder does that job instead: a package reuses your past design hours, a retainer smooths the calendar, an asset sells without you. Session 3 widens this into the other kinds of leverage that need nobody's permission.

Two neighbours sit right next to this rung. Deciding who the package is for and how it is described is positioning, taught in Marketing ↗. Setting the price from data, and how buyers respond when it moves, belongs to Economics ↗. Here a price is an input to the ledger, not a finding.

Climb one rung at a time. On the bench, each rung is added on top of the last, not swapped in for it. Keep the hourly work that pays while the first package is being built; the 64 building hours have to come from somewhere.

Build-along

One offer, one rung up

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take one piece of work you already do, ideally the one clients keep asking for, and rewrite it one rung higher as a one-page offer card. Marlow's example turns the repeated dashboard fixes into a productized "Dashboard health check". The figures in the prompts are Marlow's modelled defaults; your numbers will differ.

Name the offer and its buyer. Pick the request you get most often. Give it a name a client could repeat to a colleague, and say in one line who buys it. If you cannot name the buyer, the offer is not ready to climb.

★ Step 1 · name and buyerRepeated request: "can you look at why our dashboard is wrong / slow?" Offer name: Dashboard health check Buyer: the person who owns a BI tool at a small company Current rung: hourly, 4-8 h each time, scoped from scratch Target rung: productized

Write what is included and what is excluded. Inclusions in countable nouns. Exclusions are the things a reasonable buyer would assume are in. This is the scope line from Part 2, written once for every future sale.

★ Step 2 · in and outIncluded: audit of 1 dashboard (queries, refresh, filters, definitions) a written fix list, ranked by impact one 45-minute walk-through call Excluded: rebuilding the dashboard, adding new data sources, training the team, changes after the call Change rule: anything excluded is quoted separately

Set the price. One price, published, the same for every buyer. Marlow uses the model's default; where your price comes from, and how buyers react when it moves, is a question for Economics ↗.

★ Step 3 · pricePrice: $1,800, fixed Discounts: none; a bigger job is a different offer (Marlow's modelled default; your numbers will differ)

Count every hour the offer drags in. Delivery, the sales time to win one, and the admin slice. In the model, each client billed in a week adds half an hour of invoicing, email and onboarding; give the offer its share.

★ Step 4 · hours per saleDelivery hours 7.0 Sales hours 1.0 (call + short proposal) Admin slice 0.5 (invoice, email, onboarding) Total per sale 8.5 Building hours 64 once, before the first sale (8 h x 8 weeks)

Compute the per-offer effective rate. Price divided by every hour from Step 4, not just the delivery ones. Put it next to your hourly list rate. Leave the building hours out of this one-sale figure, and write down how many sales it takes to earn them back.

★ Step 5 · the ratePer-offer effective rate = price / (delivery + sales + admin slice) = $1,800 / (7 + 1 + 0.5) = $1,800 / 8.5 h = $211.76 per hour Hourly list rate $110 per hour billed (arithmetic on the model's defaults; your numbers will differ)

Write the done test. The moment both you and the buyer can see the job is finished. Without it, a package slowly turns back into an open-ended engagement at a fixed price.

★ Step 6 · done testDone when: the fix list is sent, and walked through on the call, and the buyer has confirmed receipt in writing. After that: follow-up questions within 7 days are included; anything later is a new health check.

Sell it three times before you polish it. The first three sales show whether the package really is the same every time. If each one needed bending, it is a fixed-scope offer wearing a product's name, and the scope line matters more than the price.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Marlow moves a job from hourly to a fixed fee of $5,400 for a planned 36 hours. What has the fixed-scope offer transferred to Marlow?

On hourly work the client pays for an overrun; on a fixed fee the owner does, through a lower rate. The higher planned rate is the payment for carrying that risk, and the scope line is how you keep it small.

2 · On the session 4 bench, adding retainers lowers Marlow's modelled effective rate from $110.93 to $108.80. Why might the retainer rung still be worth it?

It is the two-number rung: revenue rises by only $1,390 and the rate falls, while concentration drops sharply. Whether that trade is worth it depends on how exposed the business is, which session 5 measures.

3 · Clients keep asking Marlow for the same audit of their dashboards. Using the decision tree, which offer fits first?

The tree asks in order, and a repeated request is the first question. Clients returning for the same thing is not the same as needing ongoing access, and an audit that needs Marlow's hours to deliver is a package, not a copy.
