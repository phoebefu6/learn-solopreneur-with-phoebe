Leverage that does not need permission

Session 2 climbed the offer ladder. Tonight asks what lets a rung pay more than once. Leverage is the answer, and it comes in forms that differ in one blunt way: who has to agree before you can use it. A hire has to say yes to work for you, a lender has to say yes to fund you, and a piece of code or writing needs nobody's yes at all. We name the four forms, measure leverage as hours spent once against hours spent again, argue the case for staying small from both sides, and finish with an audit of your own recurring work.

Part 1 · covers the four forms of leverage, and who has to say yes

Four forms, and who has to say yes

5 min live

The most quoted modern framing of leverage comes from a tweet thread and podcast by the investor Naval Ravikant, opinion not research. It sorts leverage into forms and notices that the newest forms need no gatekeeper. That one observation is the most useful thing in it for a business of one, because a business of one has nobody to ask.

LiveLabour, capital, code, media3 min▶

Ravikant's thread opens with a line most freelancers feel in their bones: "You are not going to get rich renting out your time." It then names labour as "the oldest form of leverage": other people working on your behalf. Capital, money that works for you, is the second. The newest are "products with no marginal cost of replication", which in his telling means code and media, and these new forms, he says, "are permissionless".

Read that last word as a mechanism, not a slogan. To use labour, someone has to agree to work for you. To use capital, someone has to agree to lend it or invest it. To use code or media, nobody has to agree to anything: you build the template, write the guide or record the course, and the next copy costs you nothing. Customers still have to buy, of course. The difference is that nobody has to give you permission to make the thing.

The professional services version of labour leverage is older and better documented. David Maister's Managing the Professional Service Firm (1993), reported here from summaries rather than read, describes a firm's leverage as the ratio of junior to senior professionals, and gives the familiar labels finders, minders and grinders for the people who win, manage and do the work. A business of one has a ratio of zero juniors to one senior. That is the leverage it lacks by definition, and the reason the bottom row of the figure matters so much to it.

Self-studyWhere the framing comes from, and its limits4 min▶

The source is a tweet thread, "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)", later expanded into a podcast whose transcript sits at nav.al/rich, dated 28 December 2019. The original thread is reported as 2018; we read the transcript, not the thread. It is one investor's opinion, written as aphorisms. It is not a study and it cites none.

Three limits are worth keeping in view. First, survivorship: the famous examples of code and media leverage are the ones that worked, and the thousands of templates and newsletters that sold three copies are not in anyone's thread. Second, "no marginal cost" is about the copy, not the selling: a product nobody hears about replicates perfectly to nobody. Third, the framing says nothing about how long the build takes or how many repeats it needs to pay back. That is the arithmetic of Part 2, and it is where most permissionless projects quietly fail.

Labour leverage for a solo business usually means a subcontractor or a first hire. Employment, contracts and payroll differ by country: take local professional advice before you take on either.

Real world

The checklist that became a product. A familiar pattern: a solo bookkeeper writes the same month-end checklist for every new client, then one day tidies it into a document and starts selling it to small businesses that are not ready for a bookkeeper. Nobody had to approve it. Whether it earns anything depends on the arithmetic in Part 2, and on being found, which is a reputation question for Personal Branding ↗.

Part 2 · covers leverage as a ratio of hours in once to hours repeated

Hours in once, hours repeated

5 min live

Strip the word leverage of its glamour and it is a ratio. Hourly work is a straight line: every unit you sell costs the same hours as the last one. A built thing costs a block of hours up front and fewer hours each time after. Leverage is whatever bends your line.

LiveTwo built things on Marlow's ledger3 min▶

On the session 4 bench, Marlow's productized offer is a built thing. The model spends 64 building hours on it across weeks 1 to 8, eight a week, before a single unit sells. From week 9 each unit sells for $1,800 and costs 7 hours of delivery and 1 hour of sales. By our arithmetic on the model's inputs, that is $225 per hour of unit work, against a list rate of $110, but only once the 64 hours have been paid back by enough units. All of it is modelled, not observed.

The second built thing is automation, and here it appears only as hours moved on the ledger. The model assumes a 20 hour set-up in week 1 that cuts admin by 40 percent. Moving from rung 4 to rung 5, admin falls from 434.5 to 263.7 hours, about 171 hours by our arithmetic. Building the automation itself is a different craft, taught in Automation ↗.

Self-studyWhy billable hours went up, not leisure3 min▶

Look at the same two rungs again. Billable hours rise from 1,689 to 1,794. The hours automation freed did not become rest: in the model, there was demand waiting, so the freed admin hours were refilled with paid work. That is the honest reading of a leverage gain. Saved hours go somewhere, and if you do not decide where, the week decides for you.

This matters for your own audit. A saving only becomes money if the freed hours are refilled with paid work, and only if the demand exists. If your diagnosis from session 1 was demand-limited, freeing hours buys you time, not revenue, until something else changes. Choose the use of the hours before you build the thing that frees them.

The other two forms have their own homes. Content and media as leverage, and the reputation and platforms that make them findable, belong to Personal Branding ↗. Capital and funding, including what borrowed money costs, belong to Finance ↗.

Real world

The template used twice. A familiar pattern: a solo analyst spends a fortnight of evenings turning a client report into a reusable template, uses it for two more clients, and then the work changes shape. The template was a real asset that never reached its crossover. The lesson is not "never build"; it is to count the repeats you expect before you start, and to be honest about how often your work actually repeats.

Part 3 · covers the case for staying small, and the counter-view

Stay small, argued both ways

5 min live

If leverage without permission lets one person earn more without hiring, a fair question follows: should a business of one try to grow into something bigger at all? There is a well-known book that says no, and a counter-view that is just as reasonable. Nobody has settled it, so we hold both.

LiveThe case for staying small3 min▶

Paul Jarvis's Company of One: Why Staying Small Is the Next Big Thing for Business (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2019), reported here from reviews and catalogue records rather than read, argues against growth for its own sake. Its reviewers list four traits a company of one should have: resilience, autonomy, speed and simplicity. The core claim is a question worth asking of any growth plan: better for whom, and at what cost to the life the business was meant to fund?

That is an argument, made well, not a finding. The book is a practitioner's case built from examples and reasoning. It does not, and does not claim to, show that small businesses outperform larger ones on any measured outcome.

Self-studyThe counter-view, and why the disagreement stays open4 min▶

The counter-view is not "grow at all costs". It is narrower. A small business that depends on one big client is fragile: on the session 4 bench, Marlow's all-hourly year has 69.5 percent of revenue from the anchor client, modelled, and session 5 is entirely about what happens when that client leaves. And staying small keeps the hourly ceiling from session 1 exactly where it is, unless some form of leverage lifts it.

No study we read settles whether small is better, for whom, or by which measure. Profit, hours, stress, resilience and independence pull in different directions, and different people weight them differently. So we teach the disagreement and keep both panels in the figure the same colour. What both sides accept is the shared middle: if you want to stay small and still lift the ceiling, the leverage has to be the kind that needs no one's permission, which is code and media, not labour or capital.

Real world

The consultant who said no to an office. A familiar pattern: a solo consultant, busy for two years, is told the next step is a team and an office. They choose instead to raise prices, turn their most repeated deliverable into a fixed package, and write publicly about their method. Revenue grows more slowly than a hiring plan promised; the week stays theirs. Neither path is the right one in general. The useful move was naming what each path would cost before choosing.

Build-along

A leverage audit

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will list the work you do again and again and the things you sell, score each for hours in once, hours per repeat and how often it repeats, note who must say yes, and compute the hours one candidate would save in a year. Marlow's version runs alongside, with constructed numbers: your numbers will differ.

List what you do again and what you sell. From your session 1 ledger and your session 2 offer list: every task that recurred more than twice in a quarter, and every offer. Do not judge yet. Delivery work, admin and sales all count.

★ Step 1 · the listClient dashboard, built by hand each time Monthly data clean-up for the anchor client Invoice run and payment chasing Scoping call + proposal for small requests Productized offer (from session 2) (Marlow, constructed; your list will differ)

Score the hours. For each row: hours in once (to build a reusable version), hours per repeat (now, and with the reusable version), and how many times a year it repeats. Use your ledger, not your hopes.

★ Step 2 · hours in, hours per repeat, repeatsDashboard by hand in once 0 per repeat 12 repeats 10 Dashboard template in once 30 per repeat 4 repeats 10 Proposal template in once 5 per repeat 1 repeats 15 (constructed numbers; your numbers will differ)

Mark who must say yes. One of four answers for each row: nobody, a client, a lender, a hire. A row that needs a lender or a hire is not ruled out, but it waits on someone else's decision, and hiring or borrowing needs local professional advice before you act.

★ Step 3 · who must say yesDashboard by hand a client (each one is commissioned) Dashboard template nobody (I build it, then use it) Subcontract clean-ups a hire Data tool on credit a lender Proposal template nobody

Compute hours saved for one candidate. Pick a row marked nobody. Saved per year is repeats times the hours each repeat no longer costs, minus the hours in once. Then write what the second year looks like, when the build is already paid.

★ Step 4 · hours saved per yearCandidate: reusable dashboard template year one 10 x (12 - 4) - 30 = 50 hours saved year two 10 x (12 - 4) = 80 hours saved, if it still repeats 10 times (constructed numbers; your numbers will differ)

Decide where the freed hours go. The bench showed freed admin hours refilled with billable work. Write down what your saved hours will become: paid work if demand exists, building the next asset, or rest. Each is a fair choice; leaving it unchosen is not.

★ Step 5 · where the hours go50 hours in year one -> half to the next built thing, half to the weekly review in session 6. If refilled with paid work at a $110 list rate, 50 h would be $5,500, but only if the demand is there. (constructed; your numbers will differ. Illustrative, not financial or tax advice.)

Pick one to build and the week to start. One row, one start week, one weekly block of building hours that fits beside your current work. A plan with no start week is a wish.

★ Step 6 · the commitmentBuild: reusable dashboard template Start: the first week after the current fixed-scope project ends Pace: 6 h a week for 5 weeks = 30 h in once Check: count repeats at the session 6 weekly review

Prefer a row you already repeat. The safest leverage is a reusable version of work you have done ten times, because the repeats are evidence, not a forecast. A new product built for buyers you have not met carries a different risk: the crossover may never arrive.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which forms of leverage need nobody's permission, and why?

Labour needs someone to agree to work for you and capital needs someone to lend or invest. Code and media need no one's yes to make or to copy, which is what Ravikant's thread means by "permissionless". Customers still have to buy; nobody has to approve the making.

2 · On the modelled bench, automation cuts admin from 434.5 to 263.7 hours, yet billable hours rise from 1,689 to 1,794. Why do billable hours go up?

Automation appears on the ledger only as hours moved. In the model those hours did not become rest; demand existed, so they became billable work. If your business is demand-limited, the same saving would buy time, not revenue.

3 · What does Paul Jarvis's Company of One (2019) argument establish?

It is an argument, reported here from reviews, not a study. The counter-view, that a small business with one big client is fragile and that staying small caps the ceiling unless leverage lifts it, is just as reasonable. No study settles it, so both are held.
