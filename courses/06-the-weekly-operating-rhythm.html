The weekly operating rhythm

In a company, someone asks every week how things are going: a manager, a stand-up, a dashboard. In a business of one, nobody does, so the hours drift, the anchor client grows, and the quiet month arrives as a surprise. Tonight we build the rhythm that replaces the manager: fifteen minutes on a fixed day, five numbers from the earlier sessions, and one lever pulled and judged a week later. We finish with the question every growing business of one meets eventually: when does staying solo stop being the right shape?

Part 1 · covers the weekly review

Fifteen minutes, five numbers

5 min live

A review that takes an hour gets skipped in the first busy week. Fifteen minutes on a fixed day, the same five numbers in the same order, is short enough to keep. Each number is one the earlier sessions already taught you to compute, so the review asks for nothing new, only for looking.

LiveThe five numbers, and what each one warns you about3 min▶

Hours by kind is the session 1 ledger for the week: billable, sales, admin, building. It warns you when admin is creeping or when no building happened for a month. Effective rate, revenue divided by every hour worked, warns you when the business is getting busier without getting better. Pipeline is what came in and what you turned away; the session 4 bench prints the second number for a reason, because turned-away work is the earliest sign that a rung up is overdue.

Runway, cash on hand divided by the weekly shortfall if the biggest client stopped, is the session 5 check. It warns you before the quiet month, not during it. Top client's share over a rolling twelve months warns you when one relationship is quietly becoming the business. On the modelled all-hourly year it was 69.5 percent; nobody chose that, and a weekly glance is how you would have seen it coming.

Self-studyWhy a fixed day matters more than a perfect template2 min▶

The value of the review is the comparison with last week, and a comparison needs the same thing measured the same way at the same interval. A rough number written every Friday beats a precise one computed whenever you remember. Put the slot in the calendar as a client meeting with yourself, keep last week's card open next to this week's, and write the numbers by hand if a spreadsheet would be an excuse to tinker. How you triage the rest of your week belongs to AI Productivity ↗; the review here is only the business ledger.

Real world

The busy year that ended thin. A familiar pattern: a solo developer has the busiest year of their life, works most weekends, and at year end finds income barely above the previous year. Every week felt full, so nothing looked wrong. A weekly card would have shown the effective rate falling for months while revenue rose a little, exactly the shape the session 4 anti-lever prints for "say yes to everything".

Part 2 · covers one lever at a time

One lever a week, judged a week later

5 min live

Change three things at once and you will never know which one worked. The weekly rhythm allows one deliberate change per week, written down with what you expect it to move, and judged against the same five numbers a week later. Most levers come from the earlier sessions: a price, a rung, a piece of leverage, a cap on one client.

LiveLevers the bench has already priced3 min▶

The session 4 bench prints what several levers do to Marlow's modelled year, which is a useful way to rank them before trying one on a real week. On the all-hourly rung, the hourly price slider at $150 instead of $110 takes revenue from $144,430 to $196,950 and the effective rate from $84.12 to $114.71, on the model's generous assumption that every client stays. Adding automation on top of retainers takes the rate from $108.80 to $116.43. Saying yes to every hourly request is the lever that looks like growth and lowers the rate.

A real week will not match the model, and that is the point of the log: you write the expected direction from the bench, run one week, and record what actually moved. How demand responds to a price change is the part the model cannot know; Economics ↗ teaches how to estimate it from your own data.

Self-studyThe monthly and quarterly layers3 min▶

Monthly, rehearse the break: rerun the session 5 table and runway as if the biggest client stopped today. It takes ten minutes and keeps the number honest as clients change. Quarterly, review the rung: which offers sold, which were turned away, and whether one offer should move a rung up. Quarterly is also the moment to read your books properly; statements and cash versus profit belong to Finance ↗, and tax questions to a local professional.

A lever with no expected effect is not a lever. "Try LinkedIn more" is an activity. "Two posts this week, expect one inbound request, judge on Friday" is a lever. Positioning and channels live in Marketing ↗ and reputation in Personal Branding ↗; here only the expectation and the verdict matter.

Part 3 · covers the solo-vs-hire line

When staying solo stops being the right shape

5 min live

The first employee changes what the business is. In the official counts from session 1, a nonemployer business is one with no paid employees; one hire moves it into a different population with payroll, management and fixed costs. Maister's account of professional service firms, reported here from summaries, describes leverage as juniors working under seniors. A business of one has none of that, which is why its earlier levers were offers and leverage that need nobody's permission. The question is when that stops being enough.

LiveThe four questions, in order3 min▶

Are you hours-limited? Session 1's diagnosis. If you have idle hours, a hire adds cost to a demand problem; if you are full and cheap, a price change is faster and free. Have you tried a rung up? A productized offer or a retainer may lift the effective rate without adding a person; on the modelled bench, the rungs took the rate from $84.12 to $116.43 with the same one person. Is the work repeatable and teachable? If only you can do it, a hire becomes a second job of supervising. Can revenue carry a fixed weekly cost through a quiet quarter? The session 5 runway, with a salary added to weekly costs, answers it.

Only four yeses point at a hire. Three yeses and a no on the last question usually point at a contractor for a defined scope, which adds hours without a fixed cost. The legal line between a contractor and an employee differs by country; take local professional advice before relying on either.

Self-studyStaying solo on purpose2 min▶

Paul Jarvis's Company of One (2019), reported here from reviews, argues against growth for its own sake. Session 3 taught that argument both ways, and it applies here too: choosing not to hire is a legitimate operating decision, not a failure to grow, as long as the five numbers on the weekly card stay healthy. What is not legitimate is drifting into a hire, or out of one, without having asked the four questions.

Build-along

Your weekly rhythm, on one page

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will write the page you will open every Friday: the slot, the five numbers with this week's values, the first lever and its expectation, and the monthly and quarterly layers. Marlow's version is shown in each step with constructed values; fill yours in beside it.

Book the slot. A fixed day and time, fifteen minutes, in the calendar as a recurring meeting. Name it after the outcome, not the activity.

★ Step 1 · the slotFriday 16:00-16:15, recurring Title: "Pick next week's one lever"

Write the five numbers for this week. From the session 1 ledger and the session 5 table. Rough is fine; the same method every week is what matters.

★ Step 2 · the five numbers1 hours billable 26 · sales 3 · admin 8 · building 2 = 39 2 rate $2,860 / 39 h = $73.33 per hour worked 3 pipeline 4 requests in, 1 turned away 4 runway $18,000 cash / $1,100 weekly shortfall = 16 weeks 5 top client 61% of the last 12 months (constructed values for Marlow; your numbers will differ)

Pick one lever and write the expectation. Choose the number that worries you most and one change aimed at it. Write the direction you expect and which number will show it.

★ Step 3 · the leverWorry: top client 61%, runway 16 weeks Lever: send the "Dashboard health check" offer (session 2) to three past small clients Expect: one sale within two weeks; top-client share starts to fall next month Judge on: pipeline (number 3), then share (number 5)

Start the lever log. Three columns. Fill the first two now and the third next Friday. Never delete a row that failed; a failed lever is the most useful row in the log.

★ Step 4 · the logweek lever expected happened 41 health check to 3 clients 1 sale in 2 wks (next Friday) 42 ...

Add the monthly and quarterly lines. Two recurring reminders on top of the weekly slot, each with its question written into the title.

★ Step 5 · the other clocksMonthly, first Friday, 10 min: "If the biggest client stopped today, how many weeks?" Quarterly, last Friday, 1 h: "Which offer should move a rung up? What do the books say?"

Answer the four hire questions once, today. Write the answers at the bottom of the page and revisit them each quarter.

★ Step 6 · the hire lineHours-limited? no: 39 of 45 hours, one request turned away Tried a rung up? starting this week (step 3) Repeatable, teachable? the health check, yes Fixed cost, quiet qtr? not yet: runway is 16 weeks Verdict: stay solo; revisit next quarter

Keep the page where you work. A pinned note, the first tab of your planning sheet, or paper on the wall. A review page you have to search for is a review page you will stop opening.

Homework

For the next four Fridays

15 min a week
  1. Run the review four Fridays in a row, same five numbers, same order.
  2. Pull one lever each week and fill the third column of the log the Friday after.
  3. On the first Friday of the month, rehearse losing your biggest client and write the runway in weeks.
  4. After four weeks, write one paragraph: which number moved most, which lever did it, and which number you stopped trusting.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does the weekly rhythm allow only one deliberate lever per week?

The log turns a week into a small test. Several simultaneous changes leave no way to attribute what moved. The bench in fact combines rungs, and the time limit is a reason to keep the review short, not to limit the levers.

2 · Marlow has idle hours most weeks and is thinking about hiring a junior to "grow". What do the four questions say?

A hire, or a contractor, answers a shortage of hours. With idle hours the binding limit is demand or price. C misstates the Jarvis argument, which is reported and argued both ways, not proved.

3 · Which of these is a lever in the sense used here?

A lever is one specific change with an expected direction and the number that will show it. B and C are activities with no expectation; D is the review itself, not a change.
