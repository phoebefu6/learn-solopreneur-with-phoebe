When one client is half the business

The best client you have is often the most dangerous line on your books. They pay on time, they need no selling, and every week they take a few more of your hours. Tonight we watch that share build in Marlow's modelled year without anyone deciding it, read what US securities rules ask public companies to say about customer dependence, and then do the one calculation that turns a vague worry into a number: how many weeks you would last if that client stopped on Monday.

Part 1 · covers top-client share

How concentration builds without anyone choosing it

5 min live

The anchor client is the easiest yes in Marlow's week. No proposal, no scoping call, no new invoice set-up: the work just arrives. On the modelled bench the anchor takes whatever hours are left after everything else is booked, so in a year with nothing but hourly work, there is a lot left, and the anchor takes it.

LiveTop-client share, and top three3 min▶

Top-client share is the revenue your biggest client paid over the last 12 months, divided by all revenue in the same 12 months. On Marlow's all-hourly rung it is 69.5 percent: of every $10 that came in, almost $7 came from one place. Top-three share does the same for the three biggest clients together, and catches the business where no single client looks large but three of them are the whole year.

What the number measures is dependence on revenue, nothing more. What it misses matters just as much: how likely that client is to leave, how much notice they would give, whether their work is profitable, and whether you could replace them. A 40 percent client on a two-year contract and a 40 percent client who pays invoice by invoice have the same share and very different risk. Read the share as the size of the hole, not the chance of falling in.

Self-studyWhy the ladder dilutes it3 min▶

Climb the bench and the anchor's share falls every rung but one: 69.5 percent all hourly, 46.9 percent with fixed-scope projects, 30.8 percent with a productized offer, 18.2 percent with retainers and 18.9 percent once automation frees some admin hours. All modelled. Two things do the diluting. First, each rung brings more clients into the year: projects and packages are sold to people who would never have bought hourly time. Second, the bench gives the anchor whatever hours are left, so every hour a new offer claims is an hour the anchor no longer fills.

Retainers are the biggest single step because they spread steady weekly revenue across three different clients won in weeks 5, 14 and 30. That is why session 4 called rung 4 the two-number rung: its effective rate is a little lower than rung 3's, and its concentration is far lower. The small rise at rung 5 is the automation hours going back into the week, some of which the anchor picks up.

Nobody signs up to be dependent. Concentration is the sum of many reasonable weeks in which the easiest yes was the same client. That is why it has to be measured on a schedule: by the time it feels risky, it has usually been true for months.

Part 2 · covers what the disclosure rules read and report

Why investors are told about it

5 min live

Public companies are large, and a business of one is not. Still, the rules that govern what a US public company must tell its investors are a useful signal: when people who put money into a business want to know something in writing, it is because they treat it as a risk. Customer dependence is on that list.

LiveWhat the rule actually says3 min▶

Regulation S-K is the US rulebook for the non-financial parts of a public company's filings. Item 101(c)(1)(i) asks a company to describe "any dependence on revenue-generating activities, key products, services, product families or customers". For smaller reporting companies, Item 101(h)(4)(vi) lists "Dependence on one or a few major customers" among the things to describe. Both were read at source in 17 CFR 229.101, Regulation S-K Item 101 ↗.

Notice what the text does not do: it gives no percentage in those lines. It asks for a description of the dependence. The number people often quote, a customer at 10 percent or more of revenue, comes from a separate accounting rule, ASC 280, which requires disclosing revenue from any single customer at that size. That rule is reported here, not read: the accounting codification sits behind a login, so treat the 10 percent as a reporting trigger for large companies, not a safety line for you.

Self-studyNo threshold exists for a business of one4 min▶

No primary research on client concentration in very small firms was read for this session, and none is quoted. So any cap you set, 30 percent, 40 percent, half, is a personal policy, not a finding. Say so when you write it down. A policy is still useful: it turns a drifting share into a decision you revisit, and it tells you when to push the pipeline instead of taking one more week from the easy client.

To make the arithmetic easy to see, here is a constructed 12-month table for Marlow with small round numbers, set close to the modelled rung 1 share. It is not bench output, and your numbers will differ.

  • Anchor client: $100,000, 69.4%
  • Client B: $12,000, 8.3%
  • Client C: $10,000, 6.9%
  • Client D: $8,000, 5.6%
  • Client E: $6,000, 4.2%
  • Clients F and G: $4,000 each, 2.8% each
  • Total: $144,000. Top 1: 69.4%. Top 3: $122,000 of $144,000, 84.7%.

Seven clients, and still one of them is most of the year. A long client list can hide a short business.

Write the cap as a sentence, not a number. "Above 40 percent, I spend two hours a week on pipeline until it falls" is a policy you can follow. "Keep it under 40" is a wish.

Part 3 · covers the break button and runway

Runway, counted in weeks

5 min live

A share tells you how big the hole would be. Runway tells you how long you could stand at the edge of it. Session 4's break button stops Marlow's anchor client in week 27; here we slow that moment down and read it as cash.

LiveThe hole, the shortfall and the weeks3 min▶

On rung 1, losing the anchor client in week 27 takes $48,290 out of the modelled year. By week 26 Marlow has $27,830 in cash. After the loss, revenue falls to $935 a week against $2,000 of weekly costs, a shortfall of $1,065 a week. Divide one by the other: runway = cash on hand ÷ weekly shortfall, about 26 weeks. Half a year to replace most of a business.

From rung 2 up, the picture changes in kind, not just size. What is left after the loss still covers the $2,000 of weekly costs, so there is no shortfall and the cash stops draining. The holes keep shrinking: $36,850 on rung 2, $24,640 on rung 3, $11,550 on rung 4 and $9,130 on rung 5. All modelled.

Self-studyWhat the model refills, and what it cannot tell you3 min▶

Part of the hole refills on its own in the model: the hours the anchor freed are taken by small requests that Marlow had been turning away for lack of time. That is realistic, because busy solo businesses do decline work, and it is why the revenue after the loss is $935 a week rather than nothing. It is also an assumption. If those small requests had gone elsewhere for good, the shortfall would be larger and the runway shorter.

Runway here uses cash, not profit, and the two are different things; reading them properly belongs to Finance ↗. How much notice a client must give, and what your contract says about ending the work, depends on where you are and what you signed: take local professional advice.

Real world

The account that moved in-house. A familiar pattern: a small agency does excellent work for one large client for three years, and that client grows big enough to hire its own team. Nobody did anything wrong. The agency gets a polite email and a notice period, and discovers that the pipeline it never needed to build now has to be built in the time the notice period and the bank balance allow. Runway is that time, counted before the email arrives.

Build-along

Your concentration table and runway check

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open your invoices or accounting export for the last 12 months and your bank balance. You will list every client with what they paid, compute the shares, count your runway if the biggest one stopped, and leave with one action and a date. If you do not have clients yet, use Marlow's constructed table from Part 2 and do the same arithmetic.

List every client and what they paid in the last 12 months. Use invoices issued, from your accounting export or your sent mail. One line per client, biggest first. Include the ones who paid once.

★ Step 1 · the client listAnchor client $100,000 Client B $12,000 Client C $10,000 Client D $8,000 Client E $6,000 Client F $4,000 Client G $4,000 total $144,000 (Marlow, constructed; your numbers will differ)

Compute each share, then top 1 and top 3. Each client's revenue divided by the total. Top 1 is the first line's share; top 3 is the first three lines added up, divided by the total.

★ Step 2 · sharesAnchor $100,000 / $144,000 = 69.4% B $12,000 / $144,000 = 8.3% C $10,000 / $144,000 = 6.9% top 1 69.4% top 3 $122,000 / $144,000 = 84.7%

Write down cash on hand and weekly costs. Cash is what is in the business account today. Weekly costs are everything that goes out whether or not you work: your own pay, rent, software, insurance, set-aside for tax. Annual costs divided by 52 is fine.

★ Step 3 · cash and costscash on hand $28,000 weekly costs $2,000 (Marlow, constructed; your numbers will differ)

Compute weekly revenue without the top client. Take the top client out of the 12-month total and divide by 52. Do not assume the gap refills; if you think some of it would, write that down separately as a hope.

★ Step 4 · revenue without the top client$144,000 - $100,000 = $44,000 a year $44,000 / 52 = $846 a week against $2,000 of weekly costs

Compute your runway in weeks. The weekly shortfall is costs minus the revenue that is left. Runway is cash on hand divided by that shortfall. If what is left covers costs, write "covered" instead, as the bench does from rung 2 up.

★ Step 5 · runwayshortfall $2,000 - $846 = $1,154 a week runway $28,000 / $1,154 = about 24 weeks (the bench's rung 1 shows about 26; a different table gives a different answer, and so will yours)

Choose one action and the date you will check again. A cap written as a policy, a pipeline target, or one retainer to win. Put the recheck date in your calendar now; concentration drifts between looks.

★ Step 6 · one action, one datePolicy: above 40% top-client share (my cap, a policy, not a finding), 2 h a week go to pipeline. Action: win one retainer from client B or C this quarter. Recheck: first Monday of next month, same table.

Run it monthly, on a rolling 12 months. A single table is a photograph. Twelve of them show whether the share is drifting up while the year feels fine, and session 6 folds this check into a weekly fifteen-minute rhythm.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Two consultants each have a top-client share of 40 percent. What does that number tell you, and what does it leave out?

Top-client share is revenue from the biggest client divided by all revenue. It sizes the hole. Contract length, notice and replaceability decide how likely and how sudden the fall is, and the share cannot see any of them.

2 · Someone says "the SEC says no customer should be more than 10 percent of revenue". Based on what was read and reported, which reply is accurate?

The read text asks for a description of "any dependence on ... customers" and, for smaller reporting companies, "Dependence on one or a few major customers". The 10 percent rule is reported from ASC 280 and requires disclosure, not avoidance. No research threshold exists for a business of one; any cap is a personal policy.

3 · A constructed designer has $12,000 in cash and $3,000 of weekly costs. Without their top client, revenue would be $1,500 a week. What is their runway?

Runway is cash on hand divided by the weekly shortfall, not by total costs. A reduces nothing for the revenue that is left; D forgets that the shortfall is still positive. Only when what is left covers costs does the cash stop draining.
